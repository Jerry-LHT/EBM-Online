Task
Read one narrative article text source and extract numeric materials for one
article-local continuous result candidate.

Stage responsibility
The candidate was already proposed from article evidence. This stage reads the
provided text source and returns numeric materials that belong to, or may
support, that candidate.

This stage may do source-grounded constrained inference from text when the text
explicitly states a numeric relation or clearly states what statistic a number
represents. The goal is to convert text evidence into usable materials, not to
decide final meta-analysis fields.

Do not rediscover candidates. Do not decide final meta-analysis fields. Do not
perform free inference. Do not use medical or trial-design assumptions that are
not stated in the source. Do not calculate from ambiguous relations. Do not
normalize article labels. A material must contain a numeric value or a numeric
source expression. Pure semantic text without a numeric expression is not a
material.

Input meaning
- `candidate` describes the article-local result setting currently being
  completed.
- `field_need_context` lists downstream fields and value contracts. Use it only
  to understand what kinds of numeric materials may be useful.
- `source` is one narrative text source from the article.

How to use the candidate
Use the candidate as the semantic scope for this source. Before extracting a
material, decide whether the numeric statement or numeric relation belongs to
the same outcome, scale, score component, arm/comparator, population,
timepoint, subgroup, and statistic frame as the candidate.

If the text clearly refers to a different outcome, scale, score component,
population, timepoint, subgroup, arm, or statistic frame, do not treat that
number as direct for this candidate.

How to read this text source
Read the text as article evidence, not as a table. First identify direct numeric
statements relevant to the candidate. Then identify explicit numeric relations
or explicit statistic descriptions that may support the candidate, such as:
- mean with SD, SE, CI, range, or p-value stated in the same claim;
- a sample size tied to the same outcome/timepoint/analysis population;
- a statement that a value is baseline, post-treatment, follow-up, change from
  baseline, endpoint, or between-group effect;
- a statement that a parenthesized or nearby number is SD, SE, CI, range, or
  sample size;
- analyzed, randomized, completer, follow-up, or outcome-assessed populations
  when the text explicitly distinguishes them.

Preserve the article's own wording for outcome, scale, score component, arms,
populations, timepoints, subgroups, and denominator descriptions.

Inference boundary
You may return an inferred material only when the text makes the relation or
statistic identity explicit enough that the material follows directly from the
stated numbers and stated wording. If there is any meaningful ambiguity about
arm structure, analysis population, timepoint, subgroup, statistic identity, or
whether a number belongs to baseline, change, endpoint, or between-group
results, do not infer the missing material.

This stage may infer source-local material roles more often than new numeric
values. For example, if the text explicitly states that a parenthesized value is
the SD for the same mean, you may return that as an `sd` material. If the text
does not explicitly identify the statistic, mark the role as `unclear`.

Numeric material boundary
Extract continuous numeric materials such as mean, SD, SE, CI bounds, sample
size, analyzed total, change score, effect estimate, and p-value.

Do not convert SE, CI, p-values, effect estimates, or ranges into SD or mean at
this stage. Do not infer missing sample sizes or uncertainty values from vague
language.

Compound source expressions may be split when the source directly expresses
multiple numeric materials, such as `mean 3.9 (SD 2.0)` or `3.9 +/- 2.0`. In
that case, preserve the source expression in `value_text` and describe each
extracted value's role.

Interpreting numeric notation
Use this priority when deciding what a numeric expression means:
1. Source-explicit meaning: the sentence, paragraph, table reference, methods,
   or nearby wording explicitly defines the statistic. This always wins.
2. Source-context meaning: nearby article text makes the statistic identity
   clear enough, even if the exact numeric phrase does not repeat it.
3. Reporting convention: if the source gives no explicit or contextual
   definition and there is no conflicting signal, use common biomedical
   reporting conventions with medium or low confidence and state this basis in
   `source_meaning` or `warnings`.
4. Unclear: if several interpretations remain plausible, mark the role as
   `unclear`.

Common continuous reporting conventions:
- In continuous score or measurement results, `x (y)` usually means mean (SD)
  unless the source suggests SE, CI, IQR, range, or another statistic.
- `x +/- y` usually means mean +/- SD unless the source identifies SE, SEM, or
  another statistic.
- A treatment effect followed by a parenthesized interval, or wording such as
  95% CI, should be treated as an effect estimate with CI bounds, not SD.
- Median, IQR, and range cannot be used as mean or SD unless the source
  explicitly reports them as mean or SD.
- Preserve whether a value is baseline, endpoint, follow-up, or change from
  baseline. These are different statistic frames.

Source-grounding requirements
- Preserve source wording for outcomes, scales, score components, arms,
  populations, timepoints, and denominator descriptions.
- `source_meaning` should state what the number means in the article, not just
  where it appears.
- `source_ref.quote_or_anchor` should be a short source-grounded quote around
  the numeric expression or explicit statistic relation.
- Use `candidate_relevance: "direct"` only when the value belongs to the
  candidate's local result.
- Use `candidate_relevance: "supporting"` when the value belongs to the same
  candidate context but mainly helps interpret, derive, or validate another
  material.
- Use `candidate_relevance: "contextual"` when the value is nearby and may be
  useful context, but is not itself the candidate's local result.
- For inferred materials, make it explicit in `source_meaning` that the value or
  statistic role is inferred from an explicit textual relation and state that
  relation.
